            Method How this was measured

            How this was measured, and what it can't tell you

            Blocks

            Each line on the map is one stretch of street between two intersections, as San Francisco Public Works maps it, and is named by its hundred block, from the house numbers along it. Every other dataset on this page is joined to these stretches by the city's centerline number (CNN). Freeways and their ramps, private roads, and streets that exist only on paper are left out.

            Data

            SFMTA parking citations from the last two years, snapped to the nearest block side by their coordinates; the street sweeping schedule, parking regulations, meters and meter policies, all from data.sf.gov.

            Can I park here?

            Worked out in your browser from the posted rules for each block side, for the time and stay you pick. It can't see temporary signs that aren't in the city's data.

            Cameras

            SFMTA's speed cameras (with their posted limit, and the tickets and warnings each issued since it started, up to two years) and red-light cameras (tickets per intersection over the last two years).

            Car break-ins

            SFPD reports of theft from a vehicle and of auto parts in the last two years, at the corner SFPD places them.

            Pedestrians hit

            Traffic crashes resulting in injury (SFPD, via data.sf.gov) from the two years up to the latest in the data, where the other party was a pedestrian. A crash at an intersection counts for every block that meets there; one mid-block counts for that block. "Badly hurt" is the report's severe injury; the cause is the report's primary collision factor, in plainer words. The High Injury Network is Vision Zero's 2024 list of the streets where the most severe and fatal crashes happen.

            Police reports

            SFPD incident reports from the last two years, grouped into robbery; assault and other violence (assault, homicide, rape, sex offenses); pickpocketing and purse snatching; weapons; drug offenses; and naloxone given for an overdose (SFPD's code 51050, used when an officer gives naloxone). A report counts once per group, and supplements to a report aren't counted again. SFPD publishes each report at a nearby corner rather than the exact place, so the page shows corners, not blocks. The map shades neighborhoods by reports of violence and robbery per km of street, and a block's card compares its two corners with every other block's.

            Calls to police

            Calls from the public to SFPD's dispatch in the last two years (Law Enforcement Dispatched Calls for Service: Closed), by the call type dispatch closed it as: fights and assaults (fights with or without weapons, assault and battery, aggravated assault, stabbing); someone with a gun or knife (including shots fired, ShotSpotter alerts and shootings); robbery (including purse snatching); and threats and harassment (including stalking and indecent exposure). Left out: officers' own stops; calls marked domestic violence, elder abuse or child abuse, which are mostly at home; thefts, which are mostly shoplifting; suspicious persons, trespassers, noise, alarms and traffic; well-being checks and other calls about people in crisis; and complaints about homeless people. Calls aren't checked: for these types, about a third ended with no one there when police arrived (Sep 2024 to Sep 2026), and about 1 in 8 led to a written report. Each call is placed at a nearby intersection; calls marked sensitive have no place and are left out.

            After dark

            Between sunset and sunrise in San Francisco on that date.

            Summary

            The summary covers the same two years as the map, for the whole city. Around each place it counts the pedestrian crashes, and the police reports at corners, within 250 m as the crow flies, and compares that with the same circle around each of the city's intersections (where two or more differently named streets meet). Neighborhoods are ranked by count per km of street. The corners list counts crashes at an intersection, not those mid-block. Winter is November to January and summer is May to July. The totals per year are every pedestrian injury crash since 2015, and every police report of the kinds shown since 2018, in each full year, counted by data.sf.gov; there a report counts once even when it's more than one kind.

            Limits

            A ticket only appears when someone broke the rule, so quiet blocks look less enforced than they are. Always read the posted signs.

            Limits

            Only reported crashes with an injury are counted, and the crash data runs about two months behind. A street with few crashes may just have few people walking on it; counts aren't adjusted for foot traffic. Police reports show where reports were taken, which depends on where police patrol and on who calls or files online, as much as on where things happen. Naloxone reports only count overdoses where police gave it, a small share of all overdoses; paramedics' responses aren't published by location.

            About This page

            Made by Citina Liang, a PhD candidate in Industrial & Systems Engineering at USC Viterbi who models how people behave and how diseases spread. SF Streets is the San Francisco sister of LA Street Rules: the same map and blocks, built from San Francisco's open data, with a side for people walking. The code is on GitHub.
